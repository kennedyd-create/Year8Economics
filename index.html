The Big Question

"Who is really making the decision when you spend your money — you, or someone who wants it?"

Every day you make money decisions: what to buy, how to pay, whether a deal is real. Behind each of those choices sits a system — shops, banks, apps, advertisers and the law. Some parts of that system protect you. Other parts are built to nudge you into spending more than you meant to. Over 13 lessons you will learn how money actually moves, what debt really costs, and how to make decisions that match what you value.

⚖️

The law is on your side

New Zealand has two big consumer laws. Most people your age have never heard of them. You will finish this unit knowing exactly what they promise you.

📈

Debt is maths, not magic

"Pay later" always means "pay more later". You will calculate exactly how much more, using real NZ fees and rates.

🕵️

Design is persuasion

Countdown timers and pre-ticked boxes are not accidents. Once you can name these tricks, they stop working on you.

Why debt "snowballs"

Three things happen at once, and each one makes the next one worse.

  1. 1The amount grows. Fees and interest are added to what you already owe.
  2. 2Next month's charge is bigger. Interest is charged on the new, larger total — so you pay interest on your interest.
  3. 3Your future choices shrink. Money that could have gone on something you actually want is already promised to last month's purchase.

When you buy something from a business in New Zealand, you are not just handing over money — you are entering a deal that the law has already written most of. You get guarantees whether the shop mentions them or not, and whether or not you kept the receipt.

📗

Consumer Guarantees Act 1993

The "CGA" — about the quality of what you bought

Anything you buy from a business comes with automatic promises. Goods must be:

  • ✓Of acceptable quality — they do what they are made to do, look OK, are safe, have no defects, and last a reasonable time.
  • ✓Fit for purpose — if you told the seller what you needed it for, it has to do that job.
  • ✓As described — it matches the label, the photo and what the salesperson said.
  • ✓A reasonable price, and delivered on time and in good condition.

Services have guarantees too: they must be done with reasonable care and skill, be fit for the purpose you asked for, be finished in a reasonable time, and cost a reasonable price.

Important: A shop sign saying "No refunds" does not cancel the CGA. The law beats the sign.
📕

Fair Trading Act 1986

The "FTA" — about the truth of what you were told

The FTA makes it illegal for a business to mislead you — even by accident, and even if nothing was technically a lie.

  • ✗False claims — "100% waterproof" when it is not, or "made in NZ" when it is not.
  • ✗Fake sales — "was $200, now $100" when it was never $200.
  • ✗Hidden costs — advertising one price and adding compulsory fees at checkout.
  • ✗Fake urgency — "only 1 left!" on a screen that says that to everybody, all day.
Who enforces it? The Commerce Commission. Businesses that break the FTA can be fined heavily.
Activity · You do

The Three Rs: Repair, Replace, Refund

Which remedy you get depends on how bad the problem is — and, for minor faults, the shop gets to choose. Click each R, then use them in your mind-map.

Minor failure

Something that can be fixed reasonably easily. The shop chooses whether to repair, replace or refund — and must do it free, within a reasonable time.

Substantial failure

It is unsafe, totally different from its description, cannot reasonably be fixed, or you would never have bought it had you known. You choose: refund, replacement, or compensation.

Rights vs Responsibilities — the difference A right is something the seller must do for you. A responsibility is something you must do before you can claim that right.

Does the CGA even apply?

The law does not cover everything. Decide for each situation, then reveal the answer.

Real consumer problems do not arrive labelled. Work each one through the same three steps every time — this is exactly the method the assessment expects.

STEP 1

Which right is in question?

Acceptable quality, fit for purpose, as described — or a Fair Trading Act problem with what they were told?

STEP 2

Did the buyer meet their responsibilities?

Used as intended? Bought from a business? Not just a change of mind?

STEP 3

What is the outcome?

Minor or substantial failure — and therefore repair, replace, refund, or no claim at all.

Advertising used to be a person telling you a product exists. Now it is an entire system designed to reach you when you are bored, tired or scrolling — and to make buying feel like the obvious next move.

Activity 1 · We learn

Direct vs subconscious influence

🔊 Direct — you know you are being sold to

"50% off this weekend." "Two for one." A billboard. Your brain puts its guard up, because the sales pitch is obvious.

🌊 Subconscious — it does not feel like an ad

An influencer's "morning routine". A drink bottle placed in a music video. A brand whose logo makes you feel like the kind of person who belongs. Your guard stays down, because nothing asked you to buy anything.

Ask yourself: not "is this an ad?" but "who benefits if I believe this?"

Manaakitanga and money

In te ao Māori, manaakitanga is the practice of caring for and uplifting others — generosity, hospitality, and looking after the mana of the people around you. Applied to money, it shifts the question from "what can I get?" to "who am I responsible for?"

That is why a family might spend heavily on hosting a gathering, or send money to relatives before saving for themselves. A budget that looks "inefficient" from one set of values can be exactly right from another.

Related ideas worth knowing: kaitiakitanga (guardianship — spending in a way that protects resources for the future) and whanaungatanga (relationships and belonging).

How to use a figure properly

A number on its own proves nothing. Sandwich it:

CLAIM

Scams are a serious problem for ordinary New Zealanders, not just a rare event.

EVIDENCE

Netsafe and the Global Anti-Scam Alliance estimated New Zealanders lost around $3 billion to scams in 2025, with 82% of people encountering at least one.

EXPLANATION

This shows scams have become a normal part of daily online life, which is why knowing the red flags matters more than assuming you would never fall for one.

Watch out: the $3b figure is an estimate from a survey, while the government's own reported-loss figures are much lower because most scams are never reported. Saying which is which is what makes your writing accurate.
